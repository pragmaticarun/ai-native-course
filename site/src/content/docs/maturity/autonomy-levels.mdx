## Intuition

Both use "levels", but they answer different questions:

- **Shapiro**: how does *the engineering workflow* use AI?
- **Feng et al.**: what is *the user's role* towards *one agent*?

## Shapiro, "The Five Levels" (Jan 2026)

Six tiers numbered 0–5, modelled on the driving-automation levels:

| Level | Roughly |
|---|---|
| **L0** | Manual: AI as search, occasional tab-complete |
| **L1** | Offload discrete tasks ("write a unit test") |
| **L2** | Pair with an AI-native tool, in flow |
| **L3** | You're a manager reviewing diffs ("almost everyone tops out here") |
| **L4** | You write specs and skills, review plans, leave, and check that tests pass |
| **L5** | "Dark factory": specs in, software out |

<Aside type="caution">An opinion piece by one practitioner, not research. Useful vocabulary; don't treat it as a standard.</Aside>

## Feng, McDonald & Zhang, "Levels of Autonomy for AI Agents" (2025)

Five levels defined by the **user's role**: **L1 Operator → L2 Collaborator → L3 Consultant → L4 Approver → L5 Observer**. The paper treats autonomy as a **design choice**, separate from capability: a very capable agent can still be deployed at L2.

## Try it

Describe one of your team's AI-assisted tasks. Place it on both scales. If the levels differ, you've understood the lesson (Exercise 1 makes you do this for three teams).

## C++/Rust notes
- Feng's levels map neatly onto permission design: L4 Approver ≈ "every write syscall needs a confirmation prompt"; L5 Observer ≈ "audit log only".
